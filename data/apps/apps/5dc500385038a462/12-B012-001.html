6 The Song of Secession.
Look at those mountains: they stretch from the peaks of Sindou to the foot of Ténakourou. There is not a single home, not a single woman, who does not sing this terrifying tale from the distant ages.
Once upon a time, in the thick thickets of Karfiguélà, a doe conceived three children: the first was called Sassé, the second Bambo and the last Kaguin Magnoro. Mother Biche ruled her household with an iron fist.
At dusk, she would always return to her hut, loaded with game, the day's catch, and at the edge of the forest, she would sing this tune at the threshold of her home:
- Yèrè Yèrè da
Yèrè Yèrè Yèrè da
Yèrè Sassé né Bambo da yèrè
Sassé né Bambo da yèrè
Kaguin Magnoro dé to yéna.
Come, my children, jump with frenzy
Come Sassé and Bambo
As for you, Kaguin Magnoro
I don't want to see you, go away!
Every day, at the hour when the sunset swallows up the sun's rays, she came, she sang, she enjoyed herself and at dawn, she left alone for the savannah. And the scene was repeated from day to day, from moon to moon, from year to year. Sassé and Bambo grew fat and bloated; Kaguin Magnoro wasted away. She was so emaciated that you could see her bones and guts through her withered skin.
Then one day, at a bend in the road, the adventurous hyena caught the singer singing her secession song. She hid, got as close as possible to the hut and held back the chorus. The hyena camouflaged herself not far from there and waited impatiently for daybreak. His saliva flowed all night long. The dawn was slow in coming; at last it came. The hyena was jubilant. At last, here was Biquette. She went out and barricaded the hut. "The hyena said, rubbing her hands together, drunk with impatience. And Mother Doe disappeared into the woods. And the hyena did not wait for a single blink. She appeared in front of the hut, much more driven by appetite than by art, and tried to sing the hymn of secession with fantasy.
She managed to do so, and the two doe girls rushed to the threshold, ready to break down the barricade. Kaguin Mognoro the forsaken, Kaguin Magnoro the emaciated, shouted:
"The voice you hear is not Mother's voice. I know that voice. She is the terror of the inhabitants of the neighbourhood.
And the deer did not open the door. The hyena left in a rage, with a panting tongue, a dead eye and a resentment in her heart. She disappeared into the bush.
In the evening, Mother Doe returned from her wanderings. As usual, she sang her sinister hymn of family secession. At the sight of the feast, the two children began to feast, forgetting the incident of the day. Kaguin Magnoro approached to tell the story. A swipe from Mother Doe brought her back to the bottom of the hut where she lay sick and disjointed, more than half dead. She spent the whole night without care and rescue between the pillar beams and the canaries in the storerooms.
At that time, the hyena, like an arrow, crossed the bush at full speed, in search of the land of the blacksmiths who, as tradition says, have the art of civilisation and the power to shape iron and voices. At the blacksmiths' camp, she managed to have her voice polished and then returned to stand some distance from the hut. She waited for daybreak. The day came and the sun rose, all red, red like the left eye of the hyena. Mother doe barricaded her pen and left. There at the edge of the wood she disappeared altogether. The hyena stomped about, pawing with impatience. She was chomping at the bit. But she didn't rush off right away. She knew how to learn from the past; she waited for the sun to rise, for it to be high overhead and to decline, to decline slightly. But she did not wait any longer than that. It was already a superhuman effort for her. She would risk dying of morbid waiting. It is the waiting that kills the warrior. Then she finally ran towards the hut and in her best voice she intoned:
- Yèrè Yèrè da Yèrè
Yèrè Yèrè da Yèrè
Sassé born Bambo da yèrè
Sassé né Bambo da yèrè
Kaguin Magnoro dé do yéna.
Kaguin Magnoro, she had understood the deception but she did not have time to open her mouth. Her sisters broke down the barricade and the hyena leapt into the house. She turned the hut into a carnage and set the house on fire. Blood stained the walls. The doe girls cried out for help, but to no avail. The hyena was all stained with fresh blood. She licked her lips, forgot the hymn, laughed three times and went away.
Evening came and Mother Doe wandered from the edge, frantically singing the hymn of secession. Nothing. She sang. Nothing did. Rechanta. Nothing. Re-singing. Nothing. She approached and saw the hut all bathed in blood. She could not believe her eyes. She refused reality and dared to hope. She wanted to sing again.
Kaguin Magnoro came out: "Mother, don't sing the secession song anymore. My sisters, they are all dead. The hyena has eaten them. What will you do with me that you never wanted to see or listen to? Today, fate leaves me, alone in your hands.
Mother Doe wept. Her cry went up to heaven. It rose so high that the kites flew into the ether and the armed ducks deserted the nearby shores.
Since that time, the story has flown from house to house, flooding generations. On the evening of the births, you will hear this song in this country. The women have never forgotten it since. So have the men.
