7 How Wẽnd-Tõe married Princess Pogneere.
 
A long time ago, there lived on earth an orphan named Wẽnd-Tõe, a name taken from the popular mooré saying "Wẽnd-Tõe t ɩ waore Wẽn Waaga" ("Wẽnd-Tõe t ɩ waore wẽn waaga" means: With the power of God, the leper can play the waaga, a moaaga musical instrument that is played with the fingers. It is hard to imagine that a leper with only stumps can play this instrument, but everything is possible because of the will of God).
Wẽnd-Tõe had lost his father and mother in the interval of two dry seasons. When he was seven years old on the evening of his father's funeral, his uncle undertook to raise him. He was a man of no strength of character, of great kindness. His wife, on the other hand, was a cantankerous, mean and miserly woman. She agreed to keep the child not out of love or pity, but to make him work hard.
From that day on, Wẽnd-Tõe had no rest; he carried the water, lit the fire, washed the bowls, and searched for dead wood in the forest. From morning to night, there was no rest except when sleep, "death's little brother", put a momentary halt to his activities.
Wẽnd-Tõe did all the domestic work with dedication. In spite of this, he could not escape the admonitions of his stepmother, who soon gave him a beating that was out of all proportion. But with time, the boy was used to stoically taking the blows of the whip or the stick. What humiliated him, however, was to be subjected to insults such as:
"To Satan! Prince of demons, unbelieving genius, You have devoured your father, You have devoured your mother, You will devour no one more".
Wẽnd-Tõe knew that his father and mother were resting somewhere, in a cemetery outside the village. Every day he passed by this strange place but he never dared to stop for fear of meeting ghosts or revenants during the dark moments. But one day, exasperated by being humiliated by his stepmother's children, he went to the cemetery at dusk, determined to join his dear parents. Once there, he crossed the wood and went to sit between the two graves. Like a madman or a man possessed, he began to speak alone:
"I no longer have a father. I no longer have a mother. Sadistic death has devoured. My dear parents without an ulterior motive. And inherited a thousand grotesque mockery.
Naive and severe children play with me. The wicked women never cease to bark that I am a Komb-wẽnga (demon child), The unfortunate men never take my defence.
Father, mother; the court beats me and weighs me down. No one to cajole me. No one to ask me if I am hungry. No one to see if I am sick. No one, no one ever thinks that I Wẽnd-Tõe.
Wẽnd-Tõe has a right to rest, to entertainment, to affection, to happiness.
Soon I shall be eighteen, The age of love-making feelings. Too strong for me, poor orphan.
Father, mother, I love a girl as beautiful as the dawn. She is Princess Pogneere, the fairest of the fair. Her father, the king, has set a condition that automatically eliminates me. To marry Pogneere, you must be the best rider in the kingdom. The great competition will take place on the occasion of the "Kiuugu" festival. Without a break, all the young and teenage riders train morning and evening on the racecourse square. Father, mother, I too want to take part in this great sporting competition. But unfortunately, where can I find a good racehorse?
At the question of where to find a racehorse, the earth shook and the two graves opened, revealing a chorus of voices recommending to Wẽnd-Tõe:
"Wẽnd-Tõe! Wẽnd-Tõe! Take this millet stalk, Straddle it as a horse. When the day comes, take part in the competition.
Then the earth shook again and the two graves closed.
Wẽnd-Tõe returned home and, under the cover of darkness, he carefully kept the millet stalk on a shed until the day of the competition.
At last the day of the great event arrived. Early in the morning, all the competitors dressed in sumptuous and brightly coloured clothes. The whole village was in the public square, the old men with hairy chins took their places under the big trees, waiting for the king to arrive.
The girls, more dazzling than ever, with their striped loincloths, pearl necklaces and gold and silver jewellery in their ears, sang melodious songs in praise of the best riders of previous years. The children gambolled, shouted, and performed acrobatic movements to the rhythm of the musical instruments of the griots, excited by the festive atmosphere.
The racecourse square was packed with people and the competitors lined up. The horses, spurred on by the riders, neighed impatiently. To the astonishment of many spectators, Wẽnd-Tõe, wearing his only outfit of the occasion, an indigo-dyed sex cover, riding a millet stalk, was seen taking part in the official competition. The children burst out laughing and some even began to boo the unfortunate competitor.
At last the crowd was satisfied. His Majesty the King arrived in the company of his illustrious ministers and court advisors. The griots announced this triumphal entry. After the tributes to the king, the Widi-N aaba (head of the cavalry) gave the signal for departure to thunderous applause. The riders had to ride ten times around the racecourse. The gallop of the horses raised a thick cloud of dust that enveloped Wẽnd-Tõe. But as the main body of the pack advanced, it was noticed that instead of the millet stalk ridden by the orphan, a long tail appeared, and then legs. On the fifth lap, the spectators noticed with astonishment that Wẽnd-Tõe was racing on a real white horse. The competitors, nervously forced their horses but the closer we got to the last lap, the more the gap between Wẽnd-Tõe and the other riders grew dangerously wide. All the spectators, young and old, became obvious supporters of the orphan. From everywhere came one name: Wẽnd-Tõe! Wẽnd-Tõe!
When Wẽnd-Tõe's white horse crossed the finishing line, there was general enthusiasm. The jubilant crowd carried the orphan in triumph to the front of her majesty. The stepmother briskly pushed aside the crowd, greeted the king with her forehead to the ground, stood up and shook hands with the orphan, repeating: "God bless you! You have raised me to the rank of the great ladies of this kingdom".
The griots gave this solemn ceremony all its brilliance and beauty: the muffled rolls of the drums and tam-tams, the prolonged creaking of the violins, the melodious whistling of the drums, the exhibitions of the talented or occasional dancers.
Wẽnd-Tõe had officially become the king's son-in-law. He received many gifts in kind and in cash, animals, clothes, jewellery, cereals and cowries. Since this story, Moose children learn to ride millet stalks, convinced that they will marry a beautiful girl like Princess Pogneere.
