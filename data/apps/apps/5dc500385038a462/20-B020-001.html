10 Ingratitude.
In a village there were two men living in two neighbouring concessions. One was very poor and the other very rich. Together they built a shed between the two courtyards under which they met from time to time to talk. At some point, their wives gave birth within a few days of each other. These two babies, all boys, were always on their parents' thighs when they were chatting. They became very close while still in the cradle. They grew up together and wanted to call each other names.
The poor man's son, the first to choose, said he would be called Mane-wẽnga (Evil One). The rich man's son thought it over and decided to call himself Mane-sõngo (Benefactor). Sometimes the poor man's son would collect the rags from the rubbish heap to make himself a pair of pants. Mane-sõngo did the same and when his father wanted to stop him, he demanded that they both be given what he had to give, otherwise he would refuse his gift. They lived like this until the age of circumcision, the initiation to life.
Mane-sõngo's father took care of all the expenses. He sewed each of them big boubous and trousers. Now grown up, they decided to go into business. Their fathers approved. Mane-sõngo's father gave each of them a herd of oxen to get them started. Their mothers prepared couscous for them and they filled their specially made goat skins. They then left their parents' yards to explore other horizons and make their fortune through trade.
After several hours of walking, they became hungry and thirsty and sat down under a tree to quench their thirst before continuing. Mane-wẽnga told Mane-sõngo that they were going to trade through his bag. He did not protest. After a few days, they used up the sack of flour and the journey continued through the bush. They walked and walked for a long time and were very hungry. But Mane-wẽnga did not think of touching his flour. Mane-sõngo, seeing his strength diminishing very considerably, asked him to take off some of the flour so that they could eat. He replied that he agreed, but only on the condition that he give him an ox. Mane-sõngo agreed and gave him the ox. In return, he received a handful of flour which only made his situation worse.
To know that he had put something in his mouth, he repeated the gift of oxen many times. In doing so, he soon found himself without a single head. All had become the property of Mane-wẽnga. There was still a long way ahead of them but his companion was still not going to make him eat gifts. He demanded that he give him an eye for the same amount he received by giving an ox. He obeyed and finally gave him his last eye in order to get some of his powder of life. Blinded, he wandered about, not knowing where to go, while Mane-wẽnga went on his way peacefully. The sun fell and he spent a painful and troubled night.
The next day, he began to wander again. He heard some birds he knew calling and said, "Whenever these birds call like this, they are either on a fruit tree or they are chasing some dangerous animal. Nevertheless, I will go there. If there is fruit, I will eat. If it is the dangerous animal, let it eat me so I can have peace.
Pointing straight towards where the shouting was going on, he was pleasantly surprised to be covered by a thick, cool shadow. Still walking straight ahead, he found that he was crushing soft things under his feet. He sat down and felt around to see what they were. "These are figs," he exclaimed, picking them up at random to eat. When he had eaten well, he lifted his head as if to look at the tree and a bird flew into his right eye socket. He wanted to wipe it off. He was surprised to find that something solid was in the eye socket and was moving when he ran his hand over the soiled area. He opened his eye and saw. Happy, he raised his head again and the bird did the same. This time, instead of trying to wipe, he took his time rubbing and found both his eyes. He thought to himself that if the bird had been able to heal him, couldn't he heal other people with the same bird excrement? He filled his goat skin and continued his journey in an unknown direction, reaching a village after a few dozen kilometres of walking. He asked to see the chief's house in order to spend the night and rest and continue the next day because it was getting late and he was very tired. To his surprise, he found that his host, his first son and his first wife were all blind. He waited until he had eaten and drunk before saying to the head of the family: "Village chief, I have something that might help you".
He got a calabash half full of water and put two handfuls of the contents of his bag into it and let it settle. He took it to the chief and asked him to wash his face. The chief obeyed and recovered his sight. The same happened to the other two. The chief asked him to go and rest, "tomorrow morning we will thank you" he added. The next morning after breakfast, the chief called all the inhabitants of his village to his court. After explaining to them what the stranger had done to him, he asked them to allow him to hand over the chieftainship to the visitor and submit because he had nothing on earth to reward him.
The whole audience approved and "Mane-sõngo became the new chief. Dances were organised and animals were sacrificed. All the chief's women and wealth became his. Three years later, one evening, the inhabitants of his village saw a very big red wind coming. Those who had clothes, calabashes or other light objects outside rushed to get them into the huts. They were afraid that the wind would blow away the roofs. Little by little, the great and thick dust approached without causing any tremors.
Suddenly, we saw several herds of animals. The owner asked the chief to whom he would like to go to rest and continue the next day because it was unwise to travel with so many animals at night. He was taken there and was very well received. After the customary zom-kom, they began to talk. Mane-sõngo recognised him by his voice and asked him if he had not recognised him. Lowering his head to mature his thoughts, Mane-wẽnga remembered and could not get a single word out of his mouth.
Leaving him in his position of reflection, the chief rose to go and wash himself. When he had bathed, the dish of to was placed on the table and Mane-sõngo told a child to invite the stranger to sit at the table with them. The child called several times but got no answer. He went back to inform Mane-sõngo.
In turn, two others were commissioned without any result. The chief was furious and turned himself in. He thought that the children had not woken up his friend, who must have been very tired. Leaning on his shoulder, he tried to shake him but he fell as if his four limbs were tied. He is dead," said the chief, slowly getting to his feet.
Shame had overtaken him, leaving all his wealth to the very man he had stripped of his possessions and whose death he wanted.
So, dear brothers and sisters, let us not plunder the goods of the people to line our own pockets. One day, we will be called to account and the property will be returned to the rightful owners even if the illegal holders have to lose their lives.
