12 The price of disdain.
 
"Monkey my friend, walking on the outskirts of the city of men, I heard words. The sons of the villages that stretch from sunrise to sunset and along the path of the stars have decided that before sunrise tomorrow, the hunters will set fire to the bush to purify the spaces and slaughter the year's game. Great threat!
I wouldn't have taken the matter seriously if the millet herders had simply paid lip service. I saw them gather under the baobabs. I have seen them take down from their roofs the wineskins of pilgrimage and distant treks. I saw the old men today without advice filing daggers and talking about blood. The powder filled the bags and I saw the old muskets sneeze in the houses.
Monkey my friend, after the dawn fire, there will be no more inhabitants on the estate: neither our brothers of the burrows, nor the neighbours of the peaks.
That is why, before I go into exile, I make it my duty of friendship to recommend to you that you flee the land.
For all answer, my friend monkey passed his hand over his posterior. He crushed an old and obdurate louse hidden under his armpit, jumped on the nearest caulker tree in the neighbourhood, and made huge circumnavigations in its branches. It came back to land at the foot of the turtle like a post-winter whirlwind and said with a malicious, taunting smile
"This news is only alarming for those who do not have agility in their legs.
He jumped on a shea tree again and walked away. The tortoise, frustrated, did not ask for more. He turned his back on the fields leading to the city of men; he swept his gaze across the spaces. She looked for a distant point at the end of the horizon and went away.
"Hurry, hurry my poor legs," she said to herself, with a heavy heart, much more exhausted with fear than with real fatigue. She walked through the twilight, she walked through the night. Like an arrow, she went along the edges of the fields; she crossed in a straight line plains and mountains, caves and valleys, far ahead. She desecrated the groves, the haunts of vipers and najas; she trod the domain of lions and felines, the dens of hyenas and caracals.
The more harmless creatures such as snails, the last survivors of the wintering period and their cousins the caterpillars laughed out loud and wondered hastily where Lady Turtle was going in such a hurry.
In the sky, the stars had begun to fade, then disappear, one by one. The cries of the bush and the howls of the carnivores were heard less and less. Covered in dust, Turtle went on her merry way. In this weather, her dusty shell was no longer distinguishable from the earth. She had no energy left in her legs. She ran into a bush. A last effort and phew! On the other side, she toppled into the muddy mire of a river; she plunged so deep that she touched the fertile silt. When she came to the surface she opened her eyes. She saw a heavy red-black-brown-purple cloud rising into the serene sky. It smelled like fire.
Turtle had not lied; the bush had turned into a glowing inferno. From the earth to the sky, from the edges to the pond, the fire fuelled by the wrath of the harmattan wind rose to the sky, ran like a snake and threatened all the animals. At that moment, the monkey was seen helpless in an infernal movement: Vlam this way, vlam that way! He ran, jumped, gesticulated, flew. The tortoise saw him do it. She saw him at work, agility in his arms.... and legs. She said nothing. The monkey was the meal of the battle.
Afterwards, Lady Turtle came out and her race regenerated the land. And as the turtle sympathised with the men, she told them this story that the men tell in the camp of the circumcised: Remember. Always listen to the advice. Behind the advice there is something threatening.
