13 Tãoosa, healer.
A long time ago, there lived a great hunter who was well known for his courage and skill. His real name, Tiraoogo, had been replaced by the common name Tãoosa (= hunter).
Tãoosa loved to hunt. He loved to roam the grassy savannah where wild animals, fattened by the rich greenery of the time, abounded. Depending on his wife's wishes, he would bring back either game birds or game animals.
Over the years, Tãoosa had ended up making hunting a profession; he did little farming; instead, they traded meat by barter: smoked meat for millet, groundnuts, maize or peas. Tãoosa's family was full of life and happiness. Their child, a boy, was well fed and grew like an okra stalk on fertile land. However, after the late weaning of this son, five years passed without the hunter's wife experiencing the tender joys of a new motherhood. However, this did not disturb the peace of the household, since the "bad" people could not say that one of the spouses was sterile. The hunter was more concerned about teaching his son hunting techniques. So he taught him how to make a bow, how to set traps for hares and giant rats.
The child took a liking to this initiation and the father was proud to see his son often bring back the hare, the animal with the long ears or the giant rat, the great thief of cereals and shea kernels.
But one day, the hunter's wife wanted to eat the meat of a porcupine. As Tãoosa also enjoyed the meat of this game, he found more pleasure in going into the high forests, exploring the caves of the "Yompi" hill to wait for the late emergence of the quills. Waiting in vain until the middle of the night. Tãoosa remembered that on his way out of his house he had stumbled with his left foot, which meant that the hunt was not going to be good. So, to prevent this, he took out of his pocket a small bag made of black cotton; he untied the knot, opened the bag and took a pinch of black powder which he placed on the palate; he then climbed an old tamarind tree set up next to the cave on Yompi hill to spend the night. He took his place between three branches. Sleep came intermittently.
Suddenly, from the depths of the cave came rumblings, murmurs, plaintive voices, shrill cries. Curious to know what mystery the black hole concealed, Tãoosa listened, remembering to arm himself. By the light of the moon, he saw that in the immense courtyard of the cave a curious world was swarming: snakes, rats, porcupines, unknown animals. Funeral rites were being performed. At the end of the ritual ceremonies, the salient events of earthly life were recounted by animal family. When the eldest porcupine spoke, he attacked Tãoosa, the evil hunter who took away their share of life, joy and happiness.
Tãoosa, as a seasoned hunter, put an end to these laments by sending a poisoned arrow to the old porcupine, who arched his back to send quills and darts at his aggressor. But alas, the arrow of Tãoosa was not to be resisted. The old porcupine's cry of distress caused panic and a panic among all those present at the meeting.
Tãoosa climbed down from the tamarind tree, grabbed his drink and, proud and alert, returned home. The worries of his wife and son were transformed into overwhelming joy at the sight of the big porcupine. The Tãoosa family enjoyed themselves for three days. Then time passed. The activities continued as the days went by: the woman cleaning, the man and the son indulging in the pleasure of hunting.
Peace and happiness reigned in the family of the great hunter until the day when the son fell ill: an unknown illness, a terrible illness. The child's face was pale, his eyes were dull, his pain intolerable, he moaned, coughed and suffocated. The father used all the remedies of the great healers of the village. The pain got worse. The child, wallowing in thick blankets, cried out:
- Father, mother, save me! Father, Mother, they prick me with needles reddened by fire. Mother, I am being skewered on glowing coals. Father, my bones crack. Father, Mother, save me.
The mother was in deep distress. She cursed the alleged perpetrators of her child's evil. The father tried to hold back a tear that beaded from his left eye. So he went out, eyes glazed over, and headed for the rising sun. He walked and walked. He reached a forest with majestically beautiful trees. The birds, frightened by Tãoosa's sudden arrival, screamed, flew away and then returned to their nests. The monkeys, clinging to the branches of the trees, let out guttural cries. Everywhere there were muffled noises, crackling and shuffling. For the first time, Tãoosa, the holder of the magic formulas to ward off the evil spirits of the forest, became afraid. A whippoorwill landed in front of him. The hunter wanted to vent his anger on the little bird, which, with a sneer, feigned and flew further forward. Many times he tried to hit the mischievous bird, but it was a waste of time until the sun set. Then, tired of the long walk, irritated by the naughty whippoorwill's game. Tãoosa climbed a giant tree to rest. Sleep did not come. In the middle of the night, a glow came from a swirl of smoke followed by a great wind that shook the tall trees. The echo echoed strange calls for a general assembly. A strange world for the hunter. All the trees, large and small, were moving, walking and taking their places around a clearing. The tamarind tree on which Tãoosa was perched presided over this extraordinary meeting. He explained that in the family of animals, the man named Tãoosa had bereaved the porcupine family for the twelfth time in the interval of two moons. Tãoosa, the hunter, was riddled with all sorts of insults: "murderer, criminal, murderer, ungrateful".
Regarding man's acts of ingratitude, the old tamarind tree continued: 'Men should look after us plants. Our branches intertwine in the hot hours. With our vines they weave baskets and baskets for their wives. They gorge themselves on our juicy and tasty fruits. In spite of everything, they do not hesitate to burn us with their fateful bushfires, which are often repeated on the occasion of the big hunts.
After the intervention of the old tamarind tree, each species explained what it does to contribute to the happiness and well-being of man: some explained that their crushed leaves give milk to a mother who does not have enough to feed her baby, or that they provide fertility to a woman who is infertile. Others say that decoctions of their bark stop bloody diarrhoea, combat acute rheumatism and cure snake bites.
Tãoosa opened his eyes, listened carefully and listed all the medicinal plants, especially those that fight rheumatism.
A strong wind shook the trees again and the plants and animals returned. The cries of the birds greeted the dawn. Tãoosa got up, gathered all the medicinal plants and, taking to his heels, returned home. He cured his only son and later experimented with other recipes. The Tãoosa couple had other children, but the husband gave up hunting in favour of medicine through the secret of plants. When people asked Tãoosa why he gave up hunting, he replied:
- When hunting becomes a passion, it is dangerous. Now I have discovered the secret of animals and plants. From now on, I will hunt the disease of men.
To hunt is to take a life. Wild animals also have the right to their share of life and happiness.
To heal is to drive out disease and to give life back. In this field, the potential of plants as remedies is enormous. From now on, my happiness lies in the second.
Thus the mysteries of the hunt led Tãoosa the hunter to become a renowned healer thanks to the virtue of plants.
